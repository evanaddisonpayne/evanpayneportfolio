# Translation brief · French (fr-FR) and German (de-DE)

Evan Addison Payne's portfolio site, translated for France and Germany. You are translating as an academic linguist who is a native speaker and a published editor in the target language. The result has to read as if Evan had written it in that language himself, for the same readers he writes for in English.

## Who reads it

CMOs, VPs of Marketing and founders at B2B tech companies, plus recruiters and Heads of Talent screening for director-level roles. Smart, busy, senior, and tired of messaging workshops. In France and Germany they read English marketing jargon every day, so some industry terms stay in English when that is what a native marketer would actually say. Use your judgment, and be consistent.

## The voice (from Evan's voice guide)

Confident, quick, opinionated, a little sassy. Sharp but never cruel. Warm and witty without giving up authority. First person, direct, no hedging. Short sentences with varied rhythm; a fragment for emphasis is fine. Humor is dry and lives in the aside, never a bit, never a wink. Pieces end on a sharp line, not a summary.

Translate the voice, not the words. An English idiom that has no living equivalent should become the line a native writer with the same edge would write. If a joke dies in translation, write the joke that lives. Do not soften the opinions and do not add new ones.

## Rules that carry over into every language

These override style.

1. **Never invent or inflate facts.** No new roles, clients, numbers, dates, awards or outcomes. Every number in English appears in your version with the same value.
2. **Credit exactly as in English.** "Led" stays led, "helped make" stays helped make (never "made"). The Intercut show is only ever "an Emmy-nominated show": no show name, no categories, no nomination count.
3. **No em dashes, ever.** Also no dash used as a sentence interjection in your language (no French tiret d'incise, no German Gedankenstrich, spaced or not). Use commas, periods or parentheses. The en dash (–) is allowed only inside number and date ranges, exactly where English has one ("+15–20%", "2023–24", "2025 – now").
4. **No colons in headings or subheadings** (h1, h2, h3, card titles, article subheads).
5. **Banned words and their equivalents.** In English: delve, tapestry, realm, foster, leverage, revolutionize, groundbreaking, game-changer, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, robust, supercharge, paradigm, plethora, myriad, synergy, rockstar, ninja, growth hacker, results-driven, world-class, best-in-class. Avoid the direct equivalents too (for example FR: levier/tirer parti, révolutionner, révolutionnaire, de pointe, à la pointe, robuste, paradigme, pléthore, myriade, synergie, de classe mondiale; DE: revolutionieren, bahnbrechend, hochmodern, Spitzen-, robust, Paradigma, Plethora, Myriade, Synergie, Weltklasse, erstklassig, ergebnisorientiert). The one exception is the article's closing joke about a buyer who "needs to hear us say synergy": that word is the point of the joke, keep it.
6. **No openers, closers or filler clichés** in your language (FR: « Dans un monde en constante évolution », « En conclusion », « Au final » as a closer; DE: „In der heutigen schnelllebigen Welt", „Zusammenfassend", „Am Ende des Tages"). No fake urgency, no engagement bait.
7. **Nothing about Evan beyond what the English says.** If a translation would need a fact you don't have, keep it vague the way English is vague, and flag it in your notes.

## Address and register

Formal address to the reader: **vous** in French, **Sie** in German. Evan speaks in first person singular. Keep the warmth and the edge inside that register. If you believe the informal form would serve Evan better for one of the surfaces, keep the formal form anyway and argue for the change in your notes.

## Typography

**French (fr-FR).** Guillemets « » with a narrow no-break space (U+202F) inside. Narrow no-break space (U+202F) before ; ! ? and a no-break space (U+00A0) before : (colons are still banned in headings). Apostrophe ’. Decimal comma, non-breaking space as thousands separator, no-break space before % (« 78 % »). Write money so a French CMO reads it naturally and it still fits the big stat numerals, for example « 1,72 M$ » or « 10 M$ ». Pick one convention and use it everywhere. Months lower case (« avril 2026 »). Capitalize titles in sentence case.

**German (de-DE).** Quotes „ " (U+201E and U+201C), inner ‚ ' . Apostrophe ’ only where German needs one. Decimal comma, period or narrow space as thousands separator (pick one), space before % („78 %"). Money in a form a German CMO reads naturally that still fits the stat numerals, for example „1,72 Mio. $" or „10 Mio. $"; one convention everywhere. Watch compound length in headings, buttons and stat labels: the layout is narrow (see below).

**Both languages.**
- **No orphans.** No paragraph, heading, caption or button may end with one word alone on its last line. The English puts a no-break space ( ) between the last two words of most headings and paragraph endings. Do the same in yours: put   between the last two words of every heading, lede, paragraph ending and button that has more than two words. In German, if the last word is a long compound,   still goes before it.
- **Eyebrow separators.** Strings with "   ·   " (three spaces, middle dot, three spaces) are split on that pattern. Keep it exactly, with the same number of segments.
- **Arrows.** Keep "  →" exactly as written.
- **Placeholders** in {braces} stay as they are.
- Curly typographic quotes and apostrophes only, never straight ones, except where the English source deliberately uses a straight apostrophe (nav and footer "Let's talk"): there use your language's normal typography.

## What stays in English

- Names of people, companies and products: Evan Addison Payne, The Starr Conspiracy (TSC), hireEZ, Intercut Productions, San Francisco AIDS Foundation, AIDS/LifeCycle, Greater Bentonville Area Chamber of Commerce, University of Arkansas, Figma, Adobe Creative Suite, LinkedIn, HBO, Lollapalooza, Charli XCX, John Summit, Slayyyter, Industry (the show).

- The **Exhibit A taglines** in the article are real English taglines from real companies. Keep them in English, exactly as written. The point of the exhibit is that they are real.
- US places and their abbreviations (Chicago, IL; Bentonville, AR; Mountain View, CA).
- Industry terms where a native marketer would keep the English (for example SEO, NPS, ARR, MQL, SQL, CAC, KPI, ABM, AEO, B2B, SaaS, go-to-market, pipeline, upsell). Decide for "Work Tech" and "HR tech" and stay consistent; explain your choice in the notes.
- Degree names: give the US degree as it is ("B.S. in Business Administration"), translating only the part after it if that reads better.

## Layout limits

The design is fixed, so length matters.

- Nav links (Work, Writing, About, Resume): one short word each, ideally 10 characters or fewer.
- Nav and footer "Let's talk" button, small caps: 16 characters or fewer.
- "MENU" / "CLOSE": one word, all caps.
- Big stat numbers in case studies ("$1.72M", "+78%", "Weekly", "Fortune 500", "AEO", "B2B SaaS", "Emmy"): keep them as short as the English or shorter. Move words into the label below if needed.
- About career strip (stops): two lines of at most about 14 characters each.
- Headings may run 10 to 20 percent longer than English. Much more than that and the layout breaks.

Language code is `fr` or `de`. Read each English source in full before writing.

## Before you hand it back

Do a second full pass as the editor, reading every string aloud in your head as a native CMO would hear it. Then check each item:

- Same keys and structure as the English; numbers identical in value.
- Zero em dashes, zero interjection dashes, zero colons in headings, zero banned words or their equivalents.
-   before the last word of every heading and paragraph ending; correct spaces before punctuation (French) and correct quotes.
- Nothing invented, nothing inflated, credit wording exact.
- Layout limits respected.
- It sounds like Evan.
